# Writing style: capture it, then write from it

The script is written in the **user's** voice, not a house template. Intake question 2 collects the style; this file says how to extract it and how to write with it.

## Capturing the style
- **Samples** (best): ask for 2–3 past scripts, captions or transcripts. Read them and write the style down in words in `style-preferences.md` under `## Writing`: viewer, register and dialect, mean sentence length, how a typical hook opens, how numbers are said, person (I / you / we), humour, what the voice never does. Quote at most one short line as an example.
- **Described**: turn the six answers (viewer, language, rhythm, tone, stance, avoid-list) into the same section.
- **References**: listen to or read the narration of the reference videos and describe it the same way. Never copy their sentences.
- **Propose**: write two 15-second sample hooks in different styles, let the user pick, record the pick.

Language rules the user states (dialect forms, spelling, words to avoid, terms kept in the standard language) go in that section verbatim. For dialects without a standard spelling, keep a short table of preferred forms and update it whenever the user corrects a word. Never call dialect copy "verified"; say a native speaker should read it once.

## Writing method (apply silently, never print it)
Think from the viewer's mind for this one idea; the structure falls out and differs every run. No template, no counts to hit.
1. **One viewer.** Write to one real person who just met this story in their feed. No "dear audience", no greeting, no explaining what they know.
2. **The gap is the product.** Find what you know and the viewer does not; make it big; the hook is the first open loop, a concrete object, not a category. The first useful or surprising fact lands by 5–10 s.
3. **Line 1 sells line 2.** Each sentence makes the next one necessary. Never reveal more than the next line needs.
4. **Loops.** Open the question before answering it; never close the last loop before the next opens; open a fresh one about 60–70 % in. Never open a loop and deflate it. (`script-loop.md` has the mechanics.)
5. **Specificity.** Swap every broad phrase for a number, name or date where one exists.
6. **Simple sentences, one idea.** A 10-year-old can follow each line; simplify the sentence, never the idea. The whole idea fits in ten words or it is muddy.
7. **Cut the fluff.** One centre of gravity. A line that is interesting but not relevant goes, even a favourite. No preamble, no "why I'm making this".
8. **Preempt the live objection** in a short aside where the doubt lands.
9. **Write distinct beats** so the edit has places to cut.

Formula-mode alarms (you chose a structure before understanding the idea; the script survives swapping the subject; you can name the format; you adapted an old hook): start again from the viewer.

## Stance constraints (set at intake)
- **Report-only:** say what was said, by whom, where, and what the record shows. Attribution inside the sentence, computed numbers labelled as computed, no verdict, no closing take, no hook that decides a question the record does not.
- **Opinion allowed:** mark opinion as opinion; facts still need a source.
- **Narrow angle:** one case or question, few figures. Cut what does not serve it.

## Supplied text
Keep its idea and facts (thesis in one line, what it leaves thin). Research only the gaps, cite what you add. Then rewrite from the viewer's side instead of re-pouring it. After the script, one chat line: the idea kept, what was added, where the loops open.

## Delivery
- **In the chat**, full text, as **numbered chunks of one or two sentences** (`S01`, `S02` …) so the user can record chunk by chunk, plus a five-line note (angle, loops, weak sources, uncertain phrases, length estimate). Working copy: `runs/<id>/script.md`.
- **Second pass by a separate reviewer** (a different subagent or session, not the writer): facts against the research file, the user's language rules, stance constraints, an honest read of the method above. It fixes in place before the user sees the script.
- Technical terms with no clean word in the user's dialect stay in the standard language, written plainly; do not invent words.
